# Market position

A position is a decision about who this is for and what it replaces. Without one, every message is written from scratch and every claim is a guess about what might land.

## 1. Get the facts, not the ambitions

Ask for:

- what the business actually sells, including the parts that are hard to explain
- who it has served, and what changed for them, in their terms
- who it loses to, and why those buyers chose the other thing
- what it will not take: the work, the customers, the terms

If the user answers with adjectives ("bespoke", "premium", "innovative"), ask for the situation that makes a customer say those words.

## 2. Write the statement

| Part | Content |
|---|---|
| Who it is for | The buyer, their situation, and the event that makes them look |
| What it replaces | The alternative they use today: a competitor, a spreadsheet, an internal person, doing nothing |
| Why this | The specific reason it wins against that alternative |
| What it refuses | Work it does not take, customers it does not serve |
| Price band | Where it sits against the alternatives, and why |

Keep each row to a sentence. If a row needs a paragraph, the position is not decided yet.

## 3. Build the proof inventory

List every claim the statement makes, and beside it the thing that substantiates it: delivered work, a number with its source, or a named reference.

Delete any claim without one. Do not soften it, do not mark it "to be evidenced later". A claim that outlives its proof is how a business ends up unable to explain itself.

## 4. Make the refusals real

The refused list must be non-empty. Write down the enquiries the business should decline, and check it against what it has actually accepted. A position that refuses nothing is not a position.

## 5. Check before you finish

- every claim cites something a reader could check
- the proof inventory matches work actually delivered, not intended
- the price band is consistent with what has been quoted before
- unknowns are marked unknown, not rounded to a comfortable answer

## 6. Never

- invent a proof point, a client name, a statistic or a case study
- claim a capability the business has not delivered
- copy a competitor's claim without the work behind it
- set the price: pricing belongs to the quote, and the position only bounds it
- describe the business as anything the user would not say out loud to a customer

## 7. Hand off

- to [enquiry](../enquiry/SKILL.md) when the position has to survive contact with a real enquiry
- to the quote conversation when a price band has to become a number
