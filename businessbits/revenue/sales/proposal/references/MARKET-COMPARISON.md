# Comparing the price with the market

Use this when the buyer, or the user, asks how the price compares with the alternatives. Answer with a cost model, not an opinion.

## Choose what to compare

- **Only routes that do the same job.** A subscription that covers one part of the work is not a substitute for a build that covers three. Name the jobs the cheaper route leaves uncovered rather than implying the price is beaten.
- **Draw the set from what the buyer already uses**, plus the alternatives they would credibly consider. A table full of options they've never heard of reads as a pitch. If a cheaper route exists that they wouldn't consider, tell the user in one line and leave it out of the buyer's table.
- **Compare by the areas the proposal prices.** If the proposal prices three areas, compare each one against its own substitutes, then give one combined summary. An area with no market substitute gets a sentence saying so, not a table. Keep each comparison inside its own area: volumes and figures from one area never reappear in another area's table.
- **Anchor on the worry the buyer stated.** If they said the running fees are the concern, that is the axis the comparison measures and the line the answer opens on.
- **Check each route works in the UK, in pounds.** A platform that excludes UK sellers or bills in dollars is not a substitute however cheap it reads. Say so in one line.

## Build the model

Three layers, never one:

1. What the build costs once.
2. What each route costs a year to run: subscription, hosting, card fees.
3. What each transaction costs at the price the buyer actually sells at.

- **Per year, every year.** Give year one to year five and the cumulative total, plus a line naming what a year contains (subscription, card fees, hosting), so the arithmetic can be checked.
- **Price every rival at the tier its volume needs**, not its entry tier. Read the vendor's own pricing page, take the tier that covers the buyer's volume, and name the tier in the assumptions.
- **Read the leading rival's pricing page before claiming a gap.** Feature coverage, usage caps and channels are on the pricing page and missing from reviews.
- **Put card processing in the row that pays it.** Some platforms include it in their percentage, others bill it on top. Comparing headline prices across the two hides the difference or reverses it.
- **Model fixed per-transaction fees at the buyer's real price.** A fixed fee that is trivial on a £30 sale can be most of a £1 sale. Include postage where it applies.
- **Count the labour as well as the fee.** A route with a low percentage that needs hundreds of hours of manual listing or setup is not cheap. Every row carries its tool cost and its labour, or it comes out.
- **Never invent a cost.** Use the buyer's actual figure. Where one has to stand in, mark it as an assumption in the same place and ask for the real number.

## Lay out the table

- Lead with what each route does for the buyer, then the costs. The money closes the table as the conclusion.
- One measure per row and one value per cell. A percentage and a fixed fee go on separate rows, never blended into one per-order figure, and a row says whose fee it is.
- A bold divider row marks the change of basis, from rates per sale to money for a year, labelled as a noun ("Annual cost by volume of sales").
- Only the route that pays for a build gets two columns, year one and year two onward. Every other route keeps one.
- Columns carry the product's own name and the unit and basis of the figure. A cell with no fee is a dash. No cell carries an explanation; assumptions go in one block above the table. If an area isn't part of a route, drop the row rather than qualifying the cell.
- Don't pick one representative volume and stay quiet about it. Give the volumes the buyer actually does, or say where the number came from.

## Show payback

- Give payback against every route, including the cheapest credible one. It is the honest benchmark and usually the longest payback.
- Show it as one short block: the sales at which the build repays itself against each rival, and the year from which it is cheaper at any volume. Not a grid of break-even thresholds.
- Never publish a year-one threshold as the general claim. "The rival is cheaper below £X" is true of year one only. Give the extra cost in year one, the saving each year after, and the months to repay.

## Check the build price

Set the build price against UK freelance and agency day rates for a realistic number of days. If the build is underpriced against the market, tell the user plainly. It's the argument against discounting further.

Where a vendor quotes a throughput or a time saving, offer a timed test on the buyer's own work instead of citing it.

## Once the user approves a table

Treat it as fixed. Later requests change that table, one change at a time, in the shape already approved. Don't rebuild it from scratch or add new tables nobody asked for.
